The Brothers Karamazov

12 October 2026

Random  ·  Home

I just finished reading The Brothers Karamazov, by the legend Fyodor Dostoevsky (well actually I finished it in July, on an 11-hour flight back from the US).

Summary: this book follows a very dysfunctional family whose arguments over money and love eventually lead to a murder. Meanwhile, everyone has quite a lot to say about God, freedom, and whether people can actually be good. Very normal family activities.

My first thought is how heavy this book is. It sneaks up on you because so much of it is conversation. Two dudes start talking and suddenly they are arguing about whether the world should exist as it does. I occasionally wanted them to get to the point, then realised that the conversation was probably the point.

What interested me most was how love runs through these arguments. Even when the characters discuss religion or morality, something much more personal often comes through. They want to be understood or forgiven. Sometimes they just cannot accept that someone loves another person.

There is this line from Zosima:

“Love in action is a harsh and dreadful thing compared with love in dreams.”

Based on the context, he is speaking to a woman who imagines caring for others but worries that their ingratitude would make her stop. She can picture the sacrifice. Having to endure an unpleasant response is where the fantasy starts falling apart.

I think the book keeps returning to that difficulty. Loving humanity sounds quite manageable until you have to deal with an actual human being. Someone who interrupts you, misunderstands your intentions, and doesn’t become a better person just because you were kind to them.

Zosima’s idea of love involves continuing to care under those conditions. That is a much more demanding definition than simply feeling strongly about someone. The other person might never appreciate what you do, and you don’t get to decide how they respond.

Dmitri’s feelings are intense (gets..), but tbh his jealousy makes that intensity dangerous. Katerina’s devotion gets tangled in pride and obligation. I found both believable because their feelings don’t seem entirely false. Someone can sincerely love another person while also wanting control over how that person sees them.

Grushenka’s encounter with Alyosha offers a different possibility. A moment of compassion interrupts what could have become another attempt to hurt someone. I liked how much depends on this small interaction. They surprise each other, and for a moment, neither behaves quite as expected.

Personally, I relate to the willingness to sacrifice for love. I am also someone who would be happy to endure difficulty for a person I care about, so Katerina’s devotion felt familiar in places. But the book made me think more carefully about that instinct. Love can expose you to pain; pain alone doesn’t tell you whether you are loving someone well.

But then, Ivan brings another difficulty into it. His refusal to accept the suffering of children feels deeply compassionate, yet he struggles with loving people at close range. I don’t think the book makes that contradiction easy to resolve. Caring about suffering doesn’t necessarily mean knowing how to respond to the person beside you.

That connects, for me, to his comment about intelligence:

“The stupider one is, the closer one is to reality. The stupider one is, the clearer one is. Stupidity is brief and artless, while intelligence wriggles and hides itself. Intelligence is a knave, but stupidity is honest and straightforward.”

Quite a statement from a dude who spends so much time thinking.

I read this as Ivan becoming suspicious of what clever explanations can excuse. Someone is suffering, and an argument about some eventual greater good doesn’t remove that suffering. His objection remains painfully simple, however sophisticated the response becomes.

Still, I wouldn’t take this as proof that stupidity makes someone honest. People can be confidently wrong, and intelligence can help expose an injustice just as easily as it can explain one away. Ivan uses some pretty careful reasoning to make his own objection.

Earlier this year, I read Kafka’s Before the Law, and I kept imagining Kafka getting kind of pissed at the suggestion that simplicity brings us closer to reality. In the story, a man spends his life waiting for permission to enter the law. His request is straightforward, but the situation never becomes clear.

To me, the story makes that promise of clarity harder to believe. We can stop making clever excuses and still face something we cannot understand. The man’s sincerity doesn’t help him gain entry, and his years of trying to understand the gatekeeper don’t get him much further. That is my comparison, anyway. I wouldn’t claim Kafka and Ivan are arguing exactly the same thing.

Anyway, what I appreciate about The Brothers Karamazov is how often the characters struggle to live with the ideas they discuss. Their arguments affect how they treat each other, sometimes with consequences they seem completely unprepared for. Someone, can give a brilliant speech, and remain completely lost about how to live.
